The AI Opportunity Assessment Framework

By the Authority Solutions® Editorial Team | Published: April 2026 | Last Updated: April 2026


A Structured Framework for Identifying Where AI Creates Business Value


Most organizations struggle not with whether to adopt AI but with where to start. The technology's breadth creates analysis paralysis: AI could theoretically improve dozens of processes across every department, but resource constraints demand prioritization. Without a structured evaluation framework, organizations either chase the most visible AI trends (deploying chatbots because competitors have them, regardless of whether chatbots address their actual operational bottlenecks) or default to the loudest internal advocate's pet project (automating the CFO's reporting workflow while ignoring higher-ROI opportunities in operations).


The AI Opportunity Assessment Framework provides a systematic methodology for identifying, evaluating, and prioritizing AI use cases based on their projected business impact relative to implementation complexity. The framework produces a ranked opportunity map that directs limited resources toward the use cases most likely to deliver measurable returns.


Phase 1: Process Discovery


The assessment begins with a comprehensive inventory of business processes across all departments. This is not a technology exercise - it is an operational mapping exercise that documents what the organization actually does, how it does it, and how much each process costs in time, labor, and error correction.


For each process, capture the process name and description, the department and roles involved, the frequency of execution, the average time per execution, the current error rate, the downstream impact of process failures or delays, and any existing technology dependencies. This inventory typically reveals 50 to 100 distinct processes in a mid-size organization, creating the raw material from which AI candidates will be identified.


The discovery phase uses three data collection methods: structured interviews with department leaders and frontline staff (capturing both documented and undocumented processes), direct observation of high-frequency workflows (verifying that documented processes match actual execution), and system analytics (extracting volume, timing, and error data from existing software platforms).


Phase 2: AI Applicability Screening


Not every process is a viable AI candidate. The screening phase evaluates each discovered process against five AI applicability criteria to filter the list from 50 to 100 processes down to 15 to 25 viable candidates.

Processes with mixed results (passing three to four criteria) are flagged as conditional candidates that may become viable after preparatory work addresses their failing criteria.


Phase 3: Impact-Complexity Scoring


The viable candidates are scored on two composite dimensions: projected business impact (how much value would successful AI implementation create) and implementation complexity (how difficult and resource-intensive would implementation be).


Impact scoring combines four factors: time savings value (hours recaptured multiplied by labor cost), error reduction value (current error cost multiplied by expected reduction percentage), speed improvement value (faster execution driving improved business outcomes), and strategic alignment (does this use case advance a stated organizational priority). Each factor is scored 1 to 5, with weightings of 35 percent for time savings, 25 percent for error reduction, 20 percent for speed improvement, and 20 percent for strategic alignment.


Complexity scoring combines four factors: technical integration requirements (how many systems must connect), data preparation needs (how much data cleanup, formatting, or collection is required), organizational change requirements (how many people and workflows are affected), and risk exposure (what happens if the AI produces incorrect outputs). Each factor is scored 1 to 5, with equal 25 percent weighting.


Phase 4: Opportunity Map Construction


Plot each scored candidate on a two-dimensional matrix with impact on the Y-axis and complexity on the X-axis. The matrix produces four quadrants. Quick wins (high impact, low complexity) - implement these first. Strategic investments (high impact, high complexity) - plan these for Phase 2 deployment. Easy additions (low impact, low complexity) - implement these alongside quick wins as capacity allows. Deprioritize (low impact, high complexity) - defer or eliminate these from the roadmap.


The quick wins quadrant typically contains 3 to 5 use cases that represent the organization's optimal starting point for AI deployment. These use cases deliver fast, visible returns with minimal implementation risk - building the organizational confidence, technical infrastructure, and operational experience needed for the more complex strategic investments in Phase 2.


Frequently Asked Questions


How long does a full opportunity assessment take?


A comprehensive assessment for a mid-size organization (50 to 200 employees) typically requires 3 to 4 weeks: 1 week for process discovery, 1 week for AI applicability screening, 1 week for impact-complexity scoring, and 2 to 3 days for opportunity map construction and roadmap development. Smaller organizations can complete the assessment in 2 weeks. The primary time investment is the discovery phase - interviewing stakeholders and documenting processes - which cannot be significantly compressed without sacrificing completeness.


Can we skip the assessment and just start with an obvious use case?


You can, but you risk optimizing a local maximum while missing the global maximum. The "obvious" use case is typically the one the most vocal stakeholder advocates for - not necessarily the one with the highest organizational ROI. The assessment takes 3 to 4 weeks and frequently reveals high-impact opportunities that no one had considered, while also revealing that some "obvious" candidates have hidden complexity that would have caused implementation failure without advance preparation.


Who should lead the opportunity assessment?


The assessment leader needs three capabilities: operational understanding (ability to evaluate business processes and estimate impact), technical literacy (ability to assess AI applicability without being an AI engineer), and organizational access (authority to interview stakeholders across departments). This combination is found in operations leadership, strategy roles, or experienced external consultants. The assessment should not be led by IT alone - the technology perspective must be balanced by operational and strategic perspectives.
